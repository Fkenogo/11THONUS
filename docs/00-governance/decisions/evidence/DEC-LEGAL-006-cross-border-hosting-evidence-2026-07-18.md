# DEC-LEGAL-006 Evidence Pack — Cross-Border Firebase Hosting Position

> ⚠️ **This evidence pack is research support and is not formal legal advice.** It was prepared by an AI research process using current, primarily-official public sources, for review by the Founder and qualified Rwanda and Burundi legal counsel. It does not resolve DEC-LEGAL-006, does not select a hosting region, and does not modify the [Decision Register](../decision-register.md). Full source citations are in the companion [Source Register](DEC-LEGAL-006-DEC-TECH-005-source-register-2026-07-18.md).

## 0. Scope and Jurisdiction Baseline

11thONUS is operated and managed from **Kigali, Rwanda** (the operating jurisdiction). **Burundi is the pilot and first launch market.** No Firebase/GCP region has been selected. This pack evaluates the legal *framework* for cross-border hosting generally — it is not tied to one candidate cloud region, since Part B's technical shortlist depends on this pack's admissibility findings, not the reverse.

## 1. Operator and User Roles

**Without overstating certainty**, based on the platform's described function (a Rwanda-managed loyalty/verified-commerce platform serving Burundi pilot businesses and customers):

- **11thONUS (the Rwanda-based operator)** most likely functions as a **data controller** under both Rwanda's Law N° 058/2021 [S1] and Burundi's Loi n° 1/03 du 10 mars 2026 [S6] for the personal data it collects directly from customers and businesses (registration, purchase records, loyalty identifiers) — it determines the purposes and means of processing.
- **Participating businesses** (the Burundi pilot merchants) likely hold an **independent controller role**, or possibly a **joint-controller relationship** with 11thONUS, for the purchase and customer-relationship data they generate through the platform — this depends on exactly how much processing discretion each party has, which is not yet fixed by any TRD or contract text reviewed. **This is a genuine open question requiring counsel input**, not resolved here.
- **Google/Firebase (the cloud provider)** functions as a **data processor** (or "sub-processor," depending on the operator/business relationship above) under both frameworks for data it hosts on 11thONUS's behalf, not on its own initiative.
- Burundi pilot operations (business onboarding, in-country marketing, potential physical presence at pilot merchants) **may create a "local establishment" or representative consideration under Burundi law**, independent of where the operator's headquarters sits. Rwanda's Law N° 058/2021 similarly applies extraterritorially to processing that involves Rwanda-connected data subjects or is carried out from Rwanda [S1, Art. 3 — exact extraterritorial scope not independently re-verified in this pass; flagged for counsel confirmation].

## 2. Rwanda Requirements

Rwanda's data protection framework is **Law N° 058/2021 of 13/10/2021 relating to the protection of personal data and privacy** [S1], in force since 15 October 2021, supervised by the **National Cyber Security Authority (NCSA)** through its Data Protection Office, launched 31 March 2022 [S2].

- **Registration (Articles 29–31):** data controllers and processors must register with the NCSA before processing. The registration application must state identity, a designated contact point, representative details (if any), the categories of personal data and data subjects, processing purposes, recipient categories, transfer countries, and risk-mitigation measures. A certificate is issued within 30 working days [S3, S1].
- **Lawful processing / consent (Article 7):** consent declarations must be given "in one of the official languages that is understandable to" the data subject [S4]. **Correction (2026-07-18):** Rwanda has **four** official languages, not three. Kinyarwanda and French were recognized in the 1962 and earlier constitutional framework; English was added by the 2003 Constitution; **Kiswahili was added as Rwanda's fourth official language by the Organic Law of 8 February 2017** ("Organic Law establishing Kiswahili as an Official Language Law"), adopted to strengthen East African Community ties [S5, S14]. The governing text is one sentence: "This Organic Law establishes Kiswahili as an official language," with implementation details left to a Presidential Order [S14]. The prior version of this pack understated this list and is corrected here.
- **Privacy notice (Article 42):** controllers must inform data subjects using language that enables understanding of processing purposes, rights, and retention periods [S4].
- **Data subject rights:** access (Art. 18), objection (Art. 19), portability (Art. 20), restriction (Art. 22), erasure (Art. 23), rectification (Art. 24) — response timeframes of 30 days (60 if escalated to the NCSA) [S4].
- **Security obligations:** the law requires appropriate technical/organizational measures (general obligation referenced across the Act; specific article not independently isolated in this pass — flag for counsel).
- **Breach notification (Articles 43–45):** notify the NCSA within **48 hours** of becoming aware of a breach; submit a full report within **72 hours** covering the breach's nature, affected data categories, mitigation measures, and a communication proposal; notify affected data subjects directly if the breach poses a "high risk" to their rights/freedoms, unless a specific exception applies [S4].
- **Data retention (Article 52):** data is retained only until the processing purpose is fulfilled, with defined extensions (legal authorization, contractual necessity, crime prevention, national security, court order, tax collection, legal proceedings, authorized research, or continued subject consent). Data must be destroyed "in a manner that prevents its reconstruction" at end of retention [S4].
- **Cross-border transfer and localization — the central finding for this decision:**
  - **Article 50: data must be stored in Rwanda by default.** "The data controller or the data processor stores personal data in Rwanda." Storage outside Rwanda requires **NCSA authorization** [S4].
  - **Article 48:** transfer outside Rwanda is permitted only with (a) NCSA authorization plus proof of adequate safeguards, (b) data subject consent, (c) a specific listed ground (contract performance, vital interests, legal claims, public interest, or legitimate interests subject to an assessment), or (d) compliance with a ratified international instrument [S4].
  - **Article 49:** cross-border transfers require a **written contract** governing the transfer, and the NCSA retains authority to **prohibit or suspend** a transfer [S4].
  - **Practical consequence for DEC-TECH-005:** because no Firebase/GCP region is physically located in Rwanda, **any** cloud-hosting choice constitutes a cross-border transfer under Rwandan law and requires either (i) NCSA authorization for offshore storage (part of, or alongside, the standard controller/processor registration), or (ii) one of Article 48's alternative grounds, each documented per Article 49's written-contract requirement. This is not a one-time formality — Article 30 requires the transfer-country to be named at registration, and Rwanda's registration guidance requires notifying the NCSA within 15 working days of any change such as commencing a new cross-border transfer [S3].

## 3. Burundi Requirements

**Correction (2026-07-18): the prior version of this pack could not retrieve the primary statutory text and reported Burundi's cross-border, registration, and breach provisions as "unconfirmed." That gap is now closed.** The official PDF was successfully downloaded directly from ARCT Burundi's website [S6] (a scanned/image-only, 32-page, bilingual French/Kirundi document with no embedded text layer; no OCR tool was available locally, so all 55 articles were reviewed **visually**, page by page, from rendered page images — article numbers and page numbers are cited below for every finding). Burundi adopted its first comprehensive personal data protection statute: **Loi n° 1/03 du 10 mars 2026 portant protection des données à caractère personnel**, promulgated by President Évariste Ndayishimiye at Gitega on **10 March 2026** (Art. 55: the law enters into force on the date of promulgation) [S6].

- **Territorial scope (Art. 2, p.4):** the law applies to processing carried out by a controller or processor, **whether or not established in Burundi**, that uses processing means **located on Burundian territory** (excluding means used only for transit). If the controller/processor is not established in Burundi, it must designate a representative established in Burundi, **unless its processing is occasional**. **This is a means-based/establishment test, not an explicit "targeting" test** (i.e., the law's own text does not say it applies merely because a controller offers services to, or collects data from, Burundian residents from abroad — the stated trigger is the use of processing means physically located in Burundi). Whether 11thONUS's actual technical stack (e.g., any local payment/SMS/mobile-money integration) uses "means located in Burundi" is a fact-specific question this pack cannot resolve and **must be confirmed with Burundian counsel** — if it is triggered, and 11thONUS's Burundi pilot processing is not "occasional" (an ongoing loyalty program plausibly is not), a **Burundi-established representative would likely be required**.
- **Excluded from scope (Art. 3, p.4):** purely personal/domestic processing by an individual, and temporary technical copies made solely for network transmission purposes.
- **Definitions (Art. 4, pp.5–8):** GDPR-influenced definitions for controller, processor, consent, Data Protection Delegate (DPD), personal data, biometric data, sensitive data, personal data breach, and — critically — **"responsable majeur du traitement" (major controller)**: an entity with more than 200 employees, any public authority/national public body, or any other private legal entity that regularly and lawfully manages data necessary for safeguarding human life, establishing/exercising/defending a legal claim, emergency therapeutic/medical patient monitoring, or health research (Art. 4, 12°). **11thONUS, as a small private company whose purpose is a loyalty/verified-commerce platform (not health, vital-interest, or legal-claims data management), does not plausibly meet this "major controller" threshold** — meaning Chapter IV's heavier obligations (below) likely do not apply, though this is an inference, not a counsel-confirmed conclusion.
- **Lawful bases (Art. 8, p.10):** consent, contract necessity, legal obligation, vital interests, public-interest/official-authority tasks, and legitimate interests — a six-ground list functionally similar to Rwanda's and to GDPR Art. 6.
- **Sensitive data (Art. 9–10, pp.11–12):** processing revealing racial/ethnic origin, political/philosophical/religious opinion, trade-union membership, health data, or biometric data used to uniquely identify a person is prohibited, subject to exceptions (explicit consent unless barred by law, vital interest, non-profit-association member data, legal claims, medical emergency, health research with consent, and a public-interest exception addressable only by ministerial order for civil-status matters).
- **Processor obligations (Art. 11–13, pp.12–13):** a written contract is required between controller and processor; sub-processing requires the controller's prior written authorization; processors must support audits.
- **Cross-border transfer (Art. 15–16, pp.14–15) — the central, now-confirmed finding for this decision:** Burundi uses an **adequacy-based mechanism, not a Rwanda-style localization default.** A controller may transfer personal data to a foreign State or international organization only if that State/organization ensures a **"sufficient level of protection"** of privacy and fundamental rights (Art. 15 ¶1). A **Ministerial Order** (Minister responsible for the digital economy) establishes a list of countries considered to have a sufficient protection level (Art. 15 ¶3). **Transfers to countries not on that list remain possible** if the receiving organization adopts **adequate safeguards approved by Burundi's data protection authority** (Art. 15 ¶4). Adequacy is assessed (Art. 16) against: (1) the destination's rule of law, human-rights record, and applicable legislation including national security; (2) the existence of an effective independent data-protection authority there; and (3) its compliance with international data-protection commitments. **No Ministerial Order/adequacy list was located or reviewed in this pass — its existence, contents, and whether any Firebase/GCP candidate jurisdiction appears on it is unconfirmed and must be requested from Burundian counsel or the new authority directly.**
- **Data subject rights (Art. 17–33, pp.16–22):** information/notice at collection (Art. 17 — no language requirement specified in the text), further-processing consent (Art. 18), automated-decision-making transparency and human-review rights (Art. 19–20), notice timing (1 month) when data is not collected directly from the subject (Art. 21), a no-justification-needed right to object to direct marketing (Art. 23), right of confirmation and access with a free first copy (Art. 24–27; special handling for health data via a designated physician, Art. 32), right to erasure (Art. 28), right to rectification (Art. 29–30 — note: access/rectification requests touching state-security, defense, or public-security processing are routed through the data-protection authority as intermediary, Art. 30), right to portability with a 15-calendar-day transfer window (Art. 31–32), and a general 1-month response deadline for access/rectification/objection/erasure requests (Art. 33).
- **Major-controller-only obligations (Chapter IV, Art. 34–41, pp.23–25) — likely not applicable to 11thONUS at pilot scale (see "major controller" definition above):** registration with the data protection authority within 6 months of acquiring major-controller status (Art. 34; non-compliance can lead to a 3-month activity suspension then a permanent processing ban, Art. 35), a record of processing activities (Art. 36), mandatory Data Protection Delegate appointment (Art. 37–39), and a mandatory Data Protection Impact Assessment before any processing presenting a high risk to rights and freedoms, presumed whenever Art. 9 sensitive-category data is involved (Art. 40–41).
- **Data protection authority — name now confirmed (Art. 42, p.25):** the law establishes an oversight body formally named **"Agence de protection des données à caractère personnel"** ("Agency for the Protection of Personal Data"). Its missions, composition, organization, and operating procedures are left to a **separate implementing decree**, which was **not located or reviewed in this pass** — its existence, adoption status, and whether the Agency is operationally accepting registrations/complaints as of 2026-07-18 remains unconfirmed and should be verified directly with Burundian counsel or the Ministry of Digital Economy.
- **General controller security obligations (Chapter VI, Art. 43–44, pp.26–28):** confidentiality of processing, written staff confidentiality undertakings, and an 11-point list of required technical/organizational security measures (access control, audit trails of who accessed/modified/read data and when, physical-premises access control, protection of storage media against unauthorized reading/copying/alteration/destruction/movement, security backups, and technology-obsolescence resilience for continued data exploitability).
- **Breach notification (Art. 45–46, pp.28–29):** notify the data protection authority within **48 hours** of becoming aware of a breach posing a risk to rights and freedoms (late notification must state reasons; the notification must describe the breach's nature, likely consequences, and mitigation measures) — the same 48-hour figure as Rwanda's authority-notification deadline. Notify **affected data subjects directly within 96 hours** of becoming aware, for breaches posing a **high** risk (in clear, simple language, describing the breach, its possible consequences, mitigation measures taken, and self-protective measures the subject can take). **This 96-hour subject-notification figure is new information not present in the prior version of this pack** and differs from Rwanda's own subject-notification standard (which is not tied to a fixed hour count in Rwanda's Art. 43–45, but triggered by a "high risk" test without a stated clock).
- **Sanctions (Chapter VII, Art. 47–51, pp.29–31):** criminal penalties for unlawful processing — 3 months to 1 year imprisonment and/or a 50,000–500,000 BIF fine for individuals (doubled/escalated ranges for private legal entities: 1–20 million BIF, doubled again if committed by a "major controller"); unlawful processing of Art. 9 sensitive-category data specifically: 500,000–5,000,000 BIF (Art. 50); unjustified objection to archival/research/statistical public-interest processing: 3 months–1 year plus 50,000–500,000 BIF, or 5–10 million BIF if committed by a major controller (Art. 49). These sanctions apply in addition to, not instead of, Burundi's Cybercrime Law and general Penal Code (Art. 51).
- **Transitional/final provisions (Chapter VIII, Art. 52–55, pp.31–32) — directly relevant to timing:** processing that pre-dates the law's entry into force, carried out by the State, a public establishment, a local authority, or a private entity with a public-service mission, requires only a **declaration** (a lighter formality than full registration) to the data protection authority (Art. 52). **All processing — including 11thONUS's, as a private entity — must come into full compliance with the law within 6 months of its entry into force** (1 year for the State/public-service-mission entities) (Art. 53). Since the law entered into force on its promulgation date of **10 March 2026**, the private-sector 6-month compliance deadline falls on approximately **10 September 2026** — a compliance clock that is **already running** as of this research date (2026-07-18) for any current or near-term Burundi-directed processing, independent of whether or when DEC-LEGAL-006/DEC-TECH-005 are formally resolved.
- **Language requirement — checked directly, not found:** unlike Rwanda's Article 7, **no provision requiring notices, consent language, or contracts to be in a specific official language was located anywhere in the law's 55 articles** during this direct review. This is a **confirmed absence from the primary text**, not a research gap — a materially different (and stronger) finding than the prior version's "unconfirmed" framing. This does not by itself mean no language obligation exists anywhere in Burundian law (e.g., Loi n° 1/11 de 2009's general consumer-protection "clear information" standard was not re-reviewed in this pass — see below), and it does not override DEC-L10N-001.
- **Consumer protection (pre-existing, separate from the new data law):** Burundi's **Loi n° 1/11 de 2009** governs general consumer protection, extended in practice to e-commerce (right to clear pre-purchase information, protection against online fraud and misleading advertising), enforced by the **National Consumer Protection Commission (CNCP)** [S10]. This statute's own text was not re-reviewed in this pass; its "clear information" standard's language implications remain a counsel question.
- **Remaining Burundi gaps after this pass:** the Ministerial adequacy list's contents (or its non-existence to date); the Agency's implementing decree and current operational status; and confirmation of how "occasional" processing is interpreted for the Art. 2 representative-appointment trigger. These are narrower and more specific than the prior version's broad "cross-border mechanism unconfirmed" gap, and are now the priority items for Burundian counsel.

## 5. Transfer-Path Analysis

```
Burundi user / participating business
        │  (data entered via the 11thONUS product)
        ▼
11thONUS platform — operated and managed from Rwanda
        │  (processing, storage decision)
        ▼
Firebase/GCP hosting jurisdiction — NOT YET SELECTED
        │  (Google as processor/sub-processor)
        ▼
Authorized support or operational access (11thONUS staff, any future support vendor)
```

For each boundary:

1. **Burundi user/business → Rwanda operator.** Legal basis: likely consent at registration/purchase, plus contractual necessity (providing the loyalty service). Notice implications: Burundi's own notice/consent requirements apply directly to this leg (specific text not yet confirmed — §3). Rwanda's Article 42 notice obligation applies to the *operator's* own processing regardless of the data subject's country, since Rwanda's law governs the controller's obligations, not only Rwanda-resident data subjects (this reading is not independently verified against Rwanda's extraterritoriality article and should be confirmed by counsel).
2. **Rwanda operator → cloud hosting jurisdiction.** This is the leg Rwanda's Article 48/49/50 directly governs (§2 above): NCSA authorization or an Article 48 alternative ground, plus a written Article 49 contract, is required regardless of which specific region is eventually chosen. **This requirement exists independent of the region decision** — it is a consequence of hosting outside Rwanda at all, since no candidate Firebase/GCP region reviewed in Part B is located inside Rwanda.
3. **Cloud hosting jurisdiction → authorized support/operational access.** Notice/contractual requirements: any 11thONUS staff or future vendor accessing production data remotely is itself a form of processing/access that should be documented in the Article 49 contract's scope and in any Burundi-side equivalent once confirmed.

2b. **Rwanda operator → cloud hosting jurisdiction, Burundi leg.** Now that Burundi's mechanism is confirmed (§3), this leg is governed by Burundi Art. 15/16: the destination must be on the Ministerial adequacy list (contents unconfirmed — §3), or the receiving party must adopt safeguards approved by Burundi's new Agency (also unconfirmed whether operational — §3). **This is a materially different mechanism from Rwanda's Art. 48/49/50** (adequacy/approved-safeguards vs. authorization/alternative-ground-plus-contract) — both must be satisfied independently for the same physical hosting decision, since 11thONUS sits at the intersection of both frameworks.

**Unresolved legal questions across this path:** (a) the contents of Burundi's Ministerial adequacy list, if one currently exists, and the Agency's operational status for approving alternative safeguards (§3); (b) whether Rwanda's Article 48 "legitimate interests" ground could apply without individual NCSA authorization for a platform of 11thONUS's described scale, or whether NCSA authorization is required regardless (Article 50's default plus Article 48(a) reads as though authorization is the primary path, with the other Article 48 grounds as case-specific alternatives — but this is an interpretive reading, not a confirmed legal conclusion); (c) Firebase Authentication **does not expose a project-level location-selection control comparable to Firestore or Cloud Storage** [S11] — but the officially-documented evidence reviewed for this pack does not itself establish the precise residency of all Authentication data, so this pack does **not** assert that identity data is definitely outside any particular jurisdiction, or that it is "globally stored." **This is disclosed to counsel as an open technical question requiring direct confirmation from Google's applicable service terms, data-processing documentation, or the provider directly** — not as a settled fact bearing on Rwanda's Article 50 localization default or Burundi's adequacy mechanism, since neither can be properly assessed against Authentication until its actual residency position is confirmed; (d) whether 11thONUS's Burundi-pilot technical stack uses any "processing means located on Burundian territory" under Art. 2, which determines whether a Burundi-established representative is required (§3).

## 6. Mandatory Communication Languages

**Rwanda: finding — supported by cited authority, favorable but not unconditional.**

Rwanda's Law N° 058/2021, Article 7, requires that consent declarations be given "in one of the official languages that is understandable to" the data subject; Article 42 requires notices to use language that enables the data subject's understanding [S4]. **Correction (2026-07-18): Rwanda has four official languages — Kinyarwanda, English, French, and Kiswahili** (Kiswahili added by the 2017 Organic Law — see §2) [S5, S14]. **Because English and French are within both Rwanda's official-language list and 11thONUS's approved product-interface language scope, providing Rwanda-facing legal notices/consent in English or French appears capable of satisfying Article 7/42's letter** — but Article 7's "understandable to him or her" qualifier is data-subject-specific, not a blanket permission; for a mass-market consumer product where individual comprehension isn't verified per-user, offering Kinyarwanda (or, per the corrected official-language list, potentially Kiswahili) would materially reduce compliance risk even if not strictly mandated by the statute's text. **This is a risk-management judgment, not a settled legal conclusion — confirm with Rwandan counsel.**

**Burundi: finding — checked directly against the primary text; no explicit requirement found, but not a clean "no."**

**Correction (2026-07-18):** the primary text of Loi n° 1/03 du 10 mars 2026 was directly retrieved and reviewed in full (§3). **No provision requiring notices, consent, or contracts to be in a specific official language was found anywhere in the law's 55 articles** — this replaces the prior version's "not confirmed from available sources" framing with a stronger, directly-verified finding: the requirement is **confirmed absent from this particular statute**, not merely unresearched. This is favorable evidence, but it is not the end of the inquiry: Burundi's official languages remain Kirundi, French, and English [S12] (Kirundi spoken by roughly 98% of the population), and Burundi's separate, pre-existing consumer-protection statute (Loi n° 1/11 de 2009) requires "clear information" to consumers for online transactions [S10] — whether "clear" has been interpreted (by regulation, case law, or CNCP guidance) to require Kirundi was **not re-reviewed in this pass** and remains open.

> **Legal compliance constraint — mandatory communication language (Burundi):** No language mandate was found in the data protection law itself (confirmed by direct primary-text review). The open item is narrower than before: whether Loi n° 1/11 de 2009's "clear information" consumer-protection standard has been interpreted to require Kirundi for consumer-facing text — this requires Burundian counsel confirmation. **This finding does not itself authorize adding Kirundi, Kiswahili, or any other language to the product's interface scope** — DEC-L10N-001 remains the sole governing record for that separate question, and any change to it requires its own future governed decision.

## 8. Provisional Legal Outcome (Jurisdiction Categories, Not Regions)

This section classifies **categories of hosting jurisdiction** (not specific Firebase/GCP regions — Part B performs the technical shortlisting once this pack's admissibility findings are applied) against the evidence gathered:

| Jurisdiction category | Classification | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A jurisdiction on Burundi's Ministerial adequacy list (Art. 15 ¶3, contents unconfirmed), provided Rwanda's Art. 48/49/50 authorization and contract requirements are also satisfied | **Potentially admissible with safeguards** | Both frameworks' mechanisms are now confirmed (§2, §3) — Rwanda is authorization/contract-based, Burundi is adequacy-list/approved-safeguards-based.
